Detecting the invisible crisis beneath Bangladesh

NASA’s GRACE satellites weigh the water held in and under the land. Over the Barind Tract in the northwest it has been falling for two decades. PRAAN follows that signal to the people it pushes off the land, and to the Dhaka wards least able to take them in.

Four questions, answered from NASA data

The challenge asks what is changing, where, how much, and whether the change is what scientists call significant. Here is the short answer. The evidence follows below.

Where is it changing?
The northwest most of all

How much?

What the words mean
Trend
A steady change in one direction over the years, rising or falling. Year-to-year ups and downs are not a trend.
Sen’s slope
How fast the value changes per year. It is the middle value of the slopes between every pair of years, so one unusual year cannot pull it far.
95% confidence interval
The range the true rate of change probably lies in. If it includes zero, the data cannot rule out “no change”.
Mann-Kendall test and p-value
A test of whether later years are consistently higher (or lower) than earlier ones. The p-value is how likely a pattern this strong would be if there were really no trend. Below 0.05 we call the trend significant.
Not significant
Not the same as “no change”. It means this record cannot tell the trend apart from natural variation. Sometimes a longer record is all a trend needs.
Anomaly
The difference from a long-term average. GRACE reports water storage this way, so a negative value means less water than the 2004–2009 average.
Serial correlation
When one year resembles the next, the record holds less independent evidence than its length suggests. The test checks for this and, where it is present, demands stronger evidence.
1The signal

Water storage is stuck at a depleted level

NASA GRACE and GRACE-FO satellites (2002–2024) detect terrestrial water storage anomalies invisible to ground sensors. We thought it was drought. Then we looked underground. In the Barind Tract, surface water storage is minimal and monsoon soil moisture is seasonal — the persistent negative TWS anomaly is therefore consistent with groundwater depletion. We tested that: subtracting the soil moisture, snow and canopy water in NASA’s GLDAS model leaves a dry-season remainder that still falls significantly (−0.65 cm/yr, p = 0.017). It is also supported by BWDB/BMDA tube-well records showing water-table decline averaging 0.2–0.4 m/year across Rajshahi district (2000–2013), and over 0.6 m/year in the fastest-depleting upazilas (Aziz et al. 2015).

−1.04 cm/yr  ·  Mann-Kendall p = 0.0046
Statistically significant decline, consistent with Boro irrigation extraction. Reported on a rank-based Mann-Kendall test rather than ordinary least squares, which gives p = 0.00058 here. The raw series has lag-1 autocorrelation of +0.76, but most of that is the trend itself: once the trend is removed the Hamed-Rao check finds no significant serial correlation, so no correction was needed. Sen's slope is −1.04 cm/yr.
Rainfall was not the cause
r = −0.266, p = 0.142 (not significant)
CHIRPS rainfall shows no significant correlation with yield loss. 4 of 5 worst yield years had above-normal rainfall — irrigation buffers surface drought
GRACE-FO continuity (2019–2024)
Persistently depressed
2022 La Niña recharge was transient — TWS returned to depleted levels by 2023–24. Dry-season storage has not regained its 2002–04 level since
Why GRACE sees what rainfall gauges miss: Groundwater extraction from the Boro (dry-season) rice crop draws down the aquifer each January–May. Monsoon rainfall in June–November recharges it, but over 2002–2017 the dry-season level still fell by about a centimetre of water a year. GRACE measures this total column water storage from orbit; no ground sensor network in Bangladesh captures this at regional scale.

Is it only the Barind Tract?

A trend in one place means little without somewhere to compare it to. We ran the identical extraction — same product, same Jan–May window, same trend test — over three regions of Bangladesh.

Barind Tract · northwest
−1.04 cm/yr  ·  Mann-Kendall p = 0.0046
Significant decline, and still significant over the full 2002–2024 record (−0.56 cm/yr, p = 0.0077). The steepest loss in the country and the region this project is built around.
Coastal south · Khulna, Satkhira, Bagerhat
−0.63 cm/yr  ·  p = 0.017
Also a significant decline over 2002–2017, shallower than the Barind. With GRACE-FO added it fades to −0.13 cm/yr (p = 0.54), so we do not claim a continuing coastal decline.
Haor basin · northeast
−0.54 cm/yr  ·  p = 0.26
No significant trend. Year-to-year swings in the wettest part of Bangladesh are large enough that the slope cannot be told apart from noise, and over the full record it tilts upward instead.

See these three series, with their trend lines and tests, in the Trend Detective, case 1.

What GRACE can and cannot resolve. A mascon cell spans roughly 300 km and Bangladesh is only about 400 km across, so the country supports about three independent samples, not an arbitrary number. We tested this rather than assuming it: a fourth region centred on Dhaka returned a series differing from the haor basin by 0.00 cm with a correlation of 1.000 — one mascon cell, not two — so it is not reported as a separate result. Over the full 2002–2024 record the haor slope turns positive (+0.32 cm/yr) but at p = 0.14 that is not a significant increase, and we do not claim one.

Three satellites and a model, one mechanism

Water storage falling is a symptom. Two more satellite records over the same districts, and NASA’s GLDAS land model, point to what is causing it: rainfall and crop greenness over the June–November growing season, beside dry-season water storage.

Falling: −1.04 cm/yr

No significant trend
p = 0.063 over 39 years. Rainfall is not what is draining the aquifer.

p < 0.0001. Crops are getting greener, not failing.
GRACE minus GLDAS · what is left after soil water
Falling: −0.65 cm/yr
p = 0.017 over 2002–2017, and still significant over 2002–2024 (−0.42 cm/yr, p = 0.047). About two-thirds of the decline is not soil moisture: it is groundwater plus surface water. See it in the Trend Detective.
Read those four together. Rainfall is steady. The crop is thriving. The water is falling, and most of the fall is below the soil. That is irrigation: the Barind is sustaining its harvest by spending an aquifer that rainfall does not refill. The satellites do not just record a decline — between them they point to the most likely driver, and rule out the obvious alternative.

Methodology

Three-layer analytical framework

Each layer carries its own epistemic status — validated, literature-informed, or scenario-based. Together they form a complete early warning pipeline: from satellite signal to actionable planning output.

Layer 1 · Validated

NASA Satellite Signal

GRACE/GRACE-FO MASCON_CRI (2002–2024) · GLDAS-2.1 Noah land water, subtracted to estimate groundwater · CHIRPS rainfall (1984–2022) · Pre-Aman season (Jan–May) TWS extraction · Mann-Kendall with Sen’s slope and a Hamed-Rao correction for serial correlation · Google Earth Engine

Layer 2 · Literature-informed

Mobility Pressure Model

Layer 3 · Scenario framework

Absorption Stress Index

Wards with complete BBS 2022 records are scored on four indicators: WorldPop 2020 population density (0.35), JRC GHSL 2020 built-up surface (0.25), JRC flood risk (0.20) and BBS 2022 mean household size (0.20). Wards without a BBS match use the three-indicator form (0.40 / 0.35 / 0.25) and are mapped but not ranked. Weights are expert-defined, not fitted. Crisis Index = normalised rank(ASI) × rank(arrivals) · OCHA COD-AB ward boundaries. Tier boundaries come from Jenks natural breaks on the Crisis Index itself, not from round numbers chosen by hand. How arrivals reach individual wards: the city-level estimate is distributed in proportion to population alone. It used to be distributed as population × (1 − ASI), sending more arrivals to less-stressed wards on the assumption that low stress meant spare capacity. We tested that assumption instead of defending it, and it does not survive.

How we tested the stress index, and what failed

The obvious test — correlate the ASI with observed 2000–2020 ward population growth — gives Spearman rho = +0.267 (p = 0.020) and appears to say that stress attracts arrivals. It does not, and we do not use it. The ASI’s largest component is present-day density, and a ward is dense today partly because it grew: the outcome sits inside the predictor. Rebuilt from year-2000 inputs only, so that nothing in the predictor can have been caused by the outcome, the index predicts nothing: rho = −0.154 (p = 0.19) over 2000–2020, −0.199 (p = 0.09) over 2000–2010, −0.119 (p = 0.31) over 2010–2020. No individual component reaches significance, and median growth across baseline-stress quartiles is flat (+105%, +115%, +106%, +106%).

So the ASI measures stress, not attraction, and we allocate on population alone — the only assumption the data supports. The ranking is robust to the choice: Kafrul Ward No-14 is the top ward under the old inverse rule, the stress-weighted rule and population-only alike, and ten of the top thirteen are shared between them. A sensitivity analysis across 625 weight combinations (±10pp per indicator, renormalised) shows the top-ranked ward is unchanged in 77.1% of combinations and the top five overlap by 4.0 of 5 on average. Membership at the boundary of the top 13 (the robustness test tracks the top 13 ranks: the 11 high-concern wards and the next two) is weighting-dependent — the exact set reproduces in 11.2% of combinations, mean overlap 11.6 of 13 — and should be read as indicative rather than fixed.

Who uses this, and what do they do?

PRAAN is designed for city planners, disaster risk managers, and urban policy teams — not as a prediction engine, but as a scenario-based early warning tool.

Urban Planners
Pre-position infrastructure
High-concern wards indicate where shelter capacity, utility networks, and social services should be prioritized before stress peaks — not after arrivals overwhelm existing systems.
Disaster Risk Managers
Seasonal early warning
GRACE TWS anomalies in January–May signal Boro irrigation drawdown intensity. A worse-than-normal pre-Aman season triggers elevated mobility pressure alerts for the following months.
Aid & Service Agencies
Route resources efficiently
The Crisis Index rank identifies which wards need intervention first. Under the central scenario the 11 high-concern wards would take about 24% of projected arrivals, in proportion to their population. They are the wards where existing stress and incoming numbers combine most strongly.
Policy Makers
Evidence-based zoning
Ward-level absorption stress data supports decisions on densification limits, affordable housing zones, and climate migration corridors — grounded in satellite observation, not assumption.
Limitations

What this tool does not claim

Every layer carries a different epistemic status. These are the constraints a reviewer should hold us to.

The stress index, tested
The ASI does not predict where people went
We checked it rather than asserting it. Rebuilt from year-2000 inputs only and tested against observed WorldPop growth, the index predicts nothing: rho = −0.154 (p = 0.19) over 2000–2020, and null over both halves, with no component significant. Correlating the present-day ASI with the same growth gives rho = +0.267 (p = 0.020), but that is circular — a ward is dense today partly because it grew. So the ASI is a description of absorption stress, not a model of migrant destination choice, and arrivals are allocated on population alone.
Satellite resolution
GRACE resolves ~300 km; the Barind Tract is smaller
Mascon solutions resolve roughly 3°, which exceeds our study area. We measured how coarse: running the same variable over four regions of the country, the northeast haor basin and the central floodplain return identical series (correlation 1.000) — Sylhet and Dhaka are inside one mascon cell. Bangladesh is about 400 km across, so it supports roughly three independent samples, not four, and we report three. Read the signal as a northwest-Bangladesh water-balance trend consistent with localised groundwater depletion, not as a Barind-specific aquifer measurement.
Attribution
Total water storage, not groundwater directly
GRACE measures total terrestrial water storage. We subtract the soil moisture, snow and canopy water in NASA’s GLDAS-2.1 Noah model, and the dry-season remainder still falls significantly in the Barind. That remainder is groundwater plus surface water, GLDAS does not model irrigation, and GRACE sees a wider area than the districts GLDAS is averaged over. So attribution to groundwater remains an inference, supported by BWDB/BMDA tube-well records showing water-table decline averaging 0.2–0.4 m/yr across Rajshahi district (2000–2013), and over 0.6 m/yr in the fastest-depleting upazilas, not a direct aquifer measurement.
Mobility model
Behavioural anchor from a different kind of shock
The 36% rate comes from a monga survey — a fast-onset seasonal income shock — applied here to a slow-onset groundwater trend. The elasticity coupling the satellite signal to the mobility rate is an assumed scenario parameter, not an estimated one. These are mobility pressure scenarios, not predictions.
Ward coverage
75 of 119 wards carry complete records
GADM splits some city-corporation wards across thana boundaries, so a single ward appears as several “(Part)” polygons that match no census record by name. Dissolving those fragments and keying on ward number recovers 75 rankable wards. 44 polygons still have no match — 23 carry union names with no ward number, and GADM 4.1 predates the ward expansion, so 55 census wards have no polygon at all. Substituting satellite population was tested and rejected: WorldPop density correlates negatively with census counts, and density × area gives rank agreement of only rho = 0.20. Unmatched wards are mapped but not ranked, so none is ranked on invented population.
